v1.0.3  2026–27 Season

What the Mountain Usually Does

A crowd-powered app has one bad morning: the first one, before the crowd arrives. PeakWait has always answered that with grey text reading No reports yet, which is honest and completely useless. In 1.0.3 those lifts tell you what that hour usually looks like instead — labelled a pattern rather than a reading, and only ever to warn you about a wait, never to tell you one is clear.

The problem with being honest about nothing

Open PeakWait on the first Saturday of the season and most of the mountain is grey. Nobody has reported the Gondola yet because nobody has ridden it yet, and we would rather say so than invent a number. That rule has never moved and is not moving now.

But "we don't know" is where the app stops being useful, and it is worth being precise about why the grey is there. We do carry an official feed for roughly half our lifts, and it tells us whether a lift is running. It does not tell us, and cannot tell us, how long you will stand at the bottom of it. No resort publishes that. The grey is a wait problem, and no source we had could touch it.

A pattern, not a reading

Every skier already carries this model. Saturday at eleven is worse than Tuesday at two. The first weekend after a storm is worse than both. A six-seater clears its own queue and a slow double does not. Presidents' week is its own category of bad.

None of that is a measurement, and none of it is a secret. So the app says it out loud, in the quiet line under the lift name, where it cannot be mistaken for a report:

One row has a wait. The other has a pattern, and no colour, because colour on this list means somebody measured it.

The difference between those two rows is the whole feature. The top row was measured, so it gets the colour bar, the band and the range. The bottom row was not, so it gets none of them — just a sentence in the same grey the row has always used, and the same invitation to be the first person to report it.

Three rules, because a guess on a screen is a promise

It disappears the moment anyone reportsA prediction only ever exists in silence. One real report on that lift and the pattern is gone from the row entirely — not averaged in, not shown alongside. A person who was standing there beats a model, always, and by a wide enough margin that the model does not get a vote.
It may warn you, never reassure youIf the model thinks a lift is quiet right now, the app says nothing at all. The two mistakes are not the same size: a warning that turns out wrong costs you nothing, because you find a shorter wait than you braced for. An optimistic guess that turns out wrong costs you twenty-five minutes standing in something you were told was clear. So the model is only allowed to speak in one direction.
It only speaks for a lift we know is spinningThe pattern needs a live official reading that the lift is open. Predicting a wait for a chair that might be on a wind hold is worse than saying nothing, and it is where the two halves of our data fit together: the resort feed tells us it is running, the pattern tells us what running usually looks like at this hour.

What we tried to build and threw away

The obvious version of this feature is lift-specific. Everyone who skis knows the base-area lifts get slammed from nine to eleven while the same mountain's upper chairs sit empty, and a model that knew which lifts were which would be far better than one that only knows the hour.

We have every lift's coordinates, so we tried to derive it two ways. First by topology: a lift is a base lift if no other lift's top sits near its bottom, since you can only reach an upper lift by riding something else first. Then by trail connectivity: count the runs that feed into each lift's base, on the theory that the busiest lift is the one the most terrain funnels back to.

Both were tested against three mountains where the answer is not in dispute. Both ranked beginner carpets above Gondola One at Vail and American Eagle at Copper — two of the biggest morning waits in Colorado. The first method breaks because the learners' carpets sit at the base with their tops right next to the real base lifts. The second breaks because a lift that serves a lot of terrain is not the same thing as a lift a lot of people are standing at.

Neither is repairable with the data we have, because our lift data carries no elevations. So the feature shipped coarse: the hour, the day, the kind of lift, and the kind of day it is. It will get lift-specific when we have a season of our own history to learn from, which is what the word historical is eventually supposed to mean. Until then, a rough honest answer beats a precise invented one.

The same standard, applied everywhere else

Making a prediction visible meant auditing every screen that consumes a wait, and that audit found something worth fixing. The lift list has always refused to display a wait that is too thin or too old to trust. First Tracks, Where to ski today and the best-next-chair recommendation were reading the raw number instead, underneath that filter.

In practice that meant the app could send you across a valley on the strength of a single report it would not have been willing to print on a screen. All four surfaces now read the same filtered value, so a wait the app won't show you is also a wait it won't route you to.

Also in 1.0.3

  • Your day counts each ride once. Passive tracking could log a single ride twice, and could occasionally credit it to a neighbouring lift at a shared base. Both are fixed, and a ride is now tagged where you got on rather than where the app noticed.
  • The PeakWait Plus card in Settings reads like a card instead of a paragraph of text.
  • A quieter, more honest crowd count. Several corrections to how reports combine, including one that let a single phone's passive reading overwrite the wait a person had typed in by hand on the same device. A tap is now the floor, not a suggestion.
