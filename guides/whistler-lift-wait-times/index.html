How a Whistler wait changes through the day

A wait at Whistler moves fast. The empty chair you spotted on the last lap can be the longest wait on the hill twenty minutes later, especially on a powder morning or when the lower mountain is socked in and everyone heads up top.

  • First hour: the gondolas and the base chairs take the hit while the alpine waits for patrol to open it.
  • Late morning: the alpine lifts fill once they open, and the crowd follows the fresh snow.
  • Lunch: the quietest window of the day for the busy chairs, if you can eat early or late.

The trick is reading the wait as it is now, not as it was on your last ride up.

Check the wait from the people already standing in it

7th Heaven, Harmony, Symphony: the usual suspects

  • The alpine trio can hit 45 minutes on a weekend or powder day. Amazing terrain, not so amazing lift capacity. Lap it early or late, not at noon with everyone else.
  • When the lower mountain is socked in or unskiable, everybody funnels up top and the alpine waits spike. That's exactly when a live wait earns its keep.
  • Pow mornings: the empty lift you spotted 20 minutes ago is now the longest wait on the hill. Go by the live wait, not the last one you saw.

FAQ

How do I check Whistler lift wait times live?

Open PeakWait for live waits reported by skiers on the mountain right now, shown as Moving, Short, Busy or Long with how fresh each one is.

When are Whistler lift waits shortest?

First chair, over lunch and in the last hour of the day, and midweek outside the holidays.

Why is 7th Heaven always so busy?

Big, popular alpine terrain served by limited chair capacity, so it fills up fast, especially on powder and weekend days. Lap it first thing or late afternoon.

How does PeakWait get its wait times?

From skiers on the mountain reporting live waits, blended with movement data, shown as timestamped bands: Moving, Short, Busy, or Long.
